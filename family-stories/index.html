Why we do this

Why family stories are worth keeping

Every family has stories: how your parents met, where your name comes from, the places your grandparents knew. A Fotomigo film gathers them from your own photos and videos, so they stay with you and with the people you love, wherever they live.

Knowing where you come from

Researchers at Emory University ask children simple questions about their family, such as how their parents met and where their name comes from. One of them, Robyn Fivush, writes that the facts themselves matter less than families sharing stories about their lives (Fivush, 2016).

A separate team asked 239 US university students the same kind of questions. Those who knew more of their family's history also felt more connected to their family, and less independent of it on the study's measure (Haydon and colleagues, 2023). That study shows a link at one point in time, not a cause.

So a family film starts with the stories behind the pictures: who is in them, where they were, and what happened next. It is made to be watched together, so the stories get told again around it.

How the story is told

How a story is told matters as well as what it holds. In a trial, mothers were taught to talk with their toddlers about shared past events in rich detail. A year after the training ended, their children gave richer memories than children whose mothers had not been taught (Reese and Newcombe, 2007). That study was about conversation, not film.

We take our cue from it: each film keeps the names, places, and small details your family remembers, so there is plenty to talk about while you watch.

Listening to older generations

Older people hold stories no one else has. In an Australian pilot, high school students visited older people in aged care every week for a school term and interviewed them about their lives as oral historians. The older people were not the students' own relatives. The researchers saw rich relationships and bonding grow between young and old, though they measured no change in wellbeing (Van Bergen and colleagues, 2024). It was small: 17 students took part, compared with 12 at a school still waiting its turn.

In your own family, a film is a way to ask those questions while there is time, and to keep the answers in their own voices.

For families far apart

If your family lives in different cities or countries, a film made from your own photos and videos can be watched together on a call, shared with a grandparent overseas, and kept by every branch of the family. People who study home movies describe family screenings as a time for talk about the people and the past (Chalfen, as described by Kleinhans, 1986). Research has not yet looked at distance itself.

We make these films to keep your family's stories and voices close. The research here shows links, one trial and one small pilot. None of it is a guarantee, and we never promise more than that.
